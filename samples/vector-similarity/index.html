    03 / EXPLAIN THE CHANGE

    What changed—and why?

    Dot product multiplies matching coordinates, then adds. Cosine divides that score by both vector lengths, removing their scale.

    Dot → B wins

    q · A = 1 × 1 + 0 × 0 = 1
    q · B = 1 × 2 + 0 × 2 = 2

    Cosine → A wins

    Lengths: q = 1, A = 1, B = √8.
    A: 1 / (1 × 1) = 1
    B: 2 / (1 × √8) ≈ 0.707

    No length means no direction: cosine for (0, 0) is undefined. A valid cosine of zero instead means perpendicular vectors.

    03 / FOLLOW THE NUMBERS

    Direction and length
    are different signals.

    Let’s return to the customer’s question and follow the original numbers slowly. The two scoring rules answer different geometric questions. Dot product measures alignment with a contribution from vector lengths. Cosine similarity compares directions after accounting for those lengths.

    Below, q means the query and d means whichever document we are scoring. The small x and y labels identify the first and second coordinates. The dot between vectors names the operation; it does not mean we multiply the whole lists as ordinary numbers.

    q · d = qₓdₓ + qᵧdᵧA single number, computed from two equally sized vectors.

    Because our query has a zero second coordinate, the second product always contributes zero. That is why B’s upward component does not reduce its dot product here. Only the rightward component contributes: B has two units of it, while A has one.

    1

    Multiply, then add.

    q · A = 1 × 1 + 0 × 0 = 1
    q · B = 1 × 2 + 0 × 2 = 2

    B wins. Its component along q is larger, even though it also points away in the y direction.

    2

    Measure each vector’s length.

    The Euclidean norm is ‖d‖ = √(dₓ² + dᵧ²). Here, ‖q‖ = 1, ‖A‖ = 1, and ‖B‖ = √8 ≈ 2.828.

    3

    Remove the length advantage.

    Cosine similarity divides the dot product by both lengths.

    cos(q, A) = 1 / (1 × 1) = 1
    cos(q, B) = 2 / (1 × √8) ≈ 0.707

    Now A wins. Cosine asks how closely the directions align, regardless of their positive scale.

    THE CONNECTION

    Dot product combines alignment and magnitude. Cosine isolates alignment. Neither is automatically the right metric for every model.

    Why does dividing by length help?

    The double bars in ‖d‖ mean the vector’s length, also called its Euclidean norm. On our map, this is the straight-line distance from the origin to the arrow tip. B makes a right triangle with sides 2 and 2, so its length is √(2² + 2²) = √8, not 4.

    To normalize B, divide both coordinates by that length: B / ‖B‖ = (2/√8, 2/√8) ≈ (0.707, 0.707). The arrow still points diagonally, but now it has length 1. A and q already have length 1, so normalization leaves them unchanged.

    Take the dot product again: A still scores 1, and normalized B scores about 0.707. Both arrows now have the same length, so B cannot win by extending farther along the query’s direction. This is the same result as dividing the original dot product by the two original lengths.

    For nonzero vectors, cosine ranges from −1 to 1: the same direction gives 1, a right angle gives 0, and opposite directions give −1. A score of 0.707 is not a 70.7% chance of relevance. It is a geometric comparison; whether it predicts useful search results must be evaluated separately.

    KEEP THE EXAMPLE STRAIGHT

    The written calculations use the opening coordinates q = (1, 0), A = (1, 0), B = (2, 2). If you changed the lab, reset it to compare these steps with the display. The lab’s scores update; this worked example remains fixed.

    Go deeper: normalization, angles and projections +

    For nonzero vectors, q · d = ‖q‖ ‖d‖ cos θ. If both vectors are normalized to length 1, their dot product equals their cosine similarity. For a fixed nonzero query, equal nonzero document norms also make the two metrics produce the same ranking.

    Cosine is 1 for the same direction, 0 for perpendicular vectors and −1 for opposite directions. Those values describe geometry, not a guaranteed semantic meaning.

    The projection of d onto q is proj_q(d) = [(d · q) / (q · q)] q, provided q is nonzero. In the original example, B projects to (2, 0). Turn on the lab’s projection overlay to see the component along q and the perpendicular remainder.

    From two vectors to a search matrix +

    Stack N document vectors of dimension D as rows of a matrix X with shape (N, D). Store the query as a column with shape (D, 1). Then Xq has shape (N, 1): one dot product per document.

    The inner dimensions must match. A transpose bug can compute document-to-document scores instead of document-to-query scores. Check shapes before interpreting any ranking. For cosine, normalize each nonzero document row and the query before multiplying, with an explicit policy for zero vectors.

    05 / FIND THE EDGE CASES

    Not every vector
    has a direction.

    The zero vector (0, 0) has length zero. Its dot product with q is zero, but its cosine similarity is undefined: the formula would divide by zero.

    A REAL PIPELINE DECISION

    Validate vector dimensions and finite values before indexing. For cosine search, reject or quarantine zero embeddings and investigate how they were produced. If your application uses a fallback, name it as a policy—not as the mathematical cosine.

    A negative score is different: it is a valid result for opposing directions. An undefined score should not silently enter the ranking as zero, where it could incorrectly outrank a valid negative score.

    Questions worth pausing over

    Does cosine always give better search results? +

    No. In our toy example, it favors the document that points exactly along the query. That establishes a geometric fact, not a search-quality result. A model may be trained for a particular scoring rule, and magnitude may carry useful information. Start with the model’s documented metric and test it on real questions with known relevant documents.

    Does a bigger vector always win under dot product? +

    No. Direction matters too. With q = (1, 0), a long vector (−3, 0) scores −3, below A’s score of 1. Even positive scaling can make a negative dot product more negative. B wins the original example because its component along q is larger, not simply because it is long.

    Is a zero cosine score the same as an invalid vector? +

    No. The nonzero vector (0, 1) is perpendicular to q = (1, 0), so its cosine is exactly 0. Its denominator is 1 × 1. The vector (0, 0) has no direction, and its denominator is zero. Those are different cases and must remain different in your search pipeline.

    You can now explain the picture. The next step is to make the same promises in code: correct arithmetic, clear input rules and no silent replacement of an undefined value.

    Small glossary＋
    Vector
    An ordered list of numbers; in this lesson, a pair drawn as an arrow from the origin.
    Embedding
    A numerical representation produced by a model. Our hand-chosen vectors illustrate the mathematics only.
    Dot product
    The sum of the products of corresponding coordinates in two equally sized vectors.
    Norm
    A measure of vector size. Here we use Euclidean length: the square root of the sum of squared coordinates.
    Normalization
    Here, dividing each coordinate of a nonzero vector by its Euclidean norm to obtain length 1.
    Cosine similarity
    Dot product divided by the product of the two norms; defined only when both vectors are nonzero.
